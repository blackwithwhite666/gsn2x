# ASD-STE100 Issue 9 writing rules

Use these 53 writing rules to review text against ASD-STE100 Issue 9. Check vocabulary with the Issue 9 controlled dictionary and the applicable subject glossary.

## Section 1 — Words

| Rule | Requirement and review detail |
|---|---|
| 1.1 | Use words approved in the dictionary, or qualifying technical nouns or technical verbs. A technical noun names a specified concept in a subject field; a technical verb names a specified concept or process in a subject field. The dictionary also identifies some non-approved words and alternatives. |
| 1.2 | Use an approved dictionary word only as an approved part of speech. If an alternative has a different part of speech, change the sentence construction. Preserve meaning; do not substitute mechanically when it changes meaning. |
| 1.3 | Use an approved word only with the meaning assigned to it in the dictionary. Standard-English meanings outside that entry are not automatically permitted; for example, use a verb meaning “obey” when that is the intended meaning rather than extending “follow.” |
| 1.4 | Use only the verb forms listed in the dictionary. Use approved adjective forms; comparative and superlative forms are supplied where applicable. Adjectives formed with approved “more” and “most” need not be listed as inflections. |
| 1.5 | A word can be used as a technical noun if it names a specified subject-field concept and fits at least one of these 22 categories: official parts information; vehicles/machines and their locations; tools/support equipment and their locations; materials, consumables, unwanted material; facilities, infrastructure, logistics; systems, components, circuits, functions, configurations, parts; mathematical/scientific/engineering terms and formulas; navigation/geography; numbers, measurement units, time and symbols; quoted text; professional roles, people, groups, organizations, geopolitical entities; body parts; personal effects, food, beverages; medical terms; official documents/document parts/standards/guidelines; environmental/operational conditions; colors; damage; computer science/information/communication technology; civil/military operations; law/regulations; animals, plants, other life forms. Color words retain their adjective role despite their classification as technical nouns; do not use their comparative or superlative forms. The examples are illustrative, not a complete approved list. Use the applicable glossary or terminology database; category membership is contextual, not a blanket exemption. |
| 1.6 | A word absent from or not approved in the dictionary can be used only when it is a technical noun or part of a technical noun. It must fit a Rule 1.5 category and be approved in the company, industry, or subject field. The same word may be permitted in that term but not in ordinary prose. |
| 1.7 | Do not use a technical noun as a verb. Use it as a noun or as an adjective within another technical noun, and recast the sentence to express the action. A word that qualifies separately as a technical verb may be used as a verb under Rule 1.12. |
| 1.8 | Use the technical noun approved by the company, industry, or subject field for a system, component, part, or process. Check official parts information and the applicable glossary. |
| 1.9 | If no technical noun is already approved for the context, select one that is short (no more than three words) and easy to understand. An illustration or item index can make a shorter noun sufficient; add modifiers only when needed for identification. |
| 1.10 | Do not use regional terms, slang, or jargon as technical nouns. Select well-known words; terms understood only in a confined region or by a small group can confuse readers. |
| 1.11 | Use one technical noun consistently for the same item throughout the text. Prefer the term approved by the applicable company, industry, or subject field. |
| 1.12 | A technical verb may be used if it names a specified subject-field action/process and fits one of the four categories: manufacturing processes; computer processes/applications; applicable subject fields; or law/regulations. The examples are not exhaustive. Technical verbs must also obey Section 3. Use an accurate approved dictionary verb when possible; do not use a technical verb if an approved verb plus a suitable technical noun can express the meaning. Use only a field-correct, necessary technical verb. |
| 1.13 | Do not use a technical verb as a noun. Its past participle may be an adjective, including within a technical noun. A word that independently qualifies as a technical noun can be used as a noun in that role. |
| 1.14 | Use the American English spelling given in the dictionary unless an applicable technical-publication specification, style guide, contract, or other official directive requires a different spelling. Do not alter spelling inside quoted text. |

## Section 2 — Multi-word nouns

| Rule | Requirement and review detail |
|---|---|
| 2.1 | Keep a multi-word noun to no more than three words. Use prepositions or explain the term when that makes its relationships clearer. |
| 2.2 | If a technical noun has more than three words, write it in full the first time. Then make it clear by explaining a shorter form, using an applicable official abbreviation, or using hyphens between words that function as one unit. Preserve official terms and their official hyphens. Do not hyphenate unrelated words or use hyphens to turn a group of more than three words into a long, hard-to-read unit. Avoid abbreviation-heavy procedure text; terms of three words or fewer do not need abbreviations. |

## Section 3 — Verbs

| Rule | Requirement and review detail |
|---|---|
| 3.1 | Use only verb forms given in the dictionary. For a technical verb, verify that its forms follow the applicable Section 3 requirements and the accepted subject terminology. |
| 3.2 | Use only the listed verb forms and tenses: infinitive, imperative, simple present, simple past, simple future, and past participle as an adjective. Do not use other forms or tenses, including perfect and progressive forms or other complex verb constructions. |
| 3.3 | Use the approved past participle as an adjective before a noun or after a form of “be,” “become,” or “stay.” It describes a condition and is not passive voice. Do not use a past participle as an adjective unless that form is in the dictionary; an adjective can be approved even when the related verb is not. |
| 3.4 | Do not use auxiliary verbs to form complex verb constructions. Recast the sentence with a permitted verb form and preserve the intended timing and meaning. |
| 3.5 | Use an “-ing” verb form only as a technical noun or as a modifier within a technical noun. Do not use verb “-ing” forms as gerunds or progressive verbs. Separately approved dictionary words ending in “-ing” remain permitted in their approved parts of speech, meanings, and forms; they need not qualify as technical terms. These include approved nouns, adjectives, a pronoun, and a preposition. |
| 3.6 | Use the active voice. In descriptive writing only, passive voice is permitted when the agent (the person or thing that does the action) is unknown. This exception does not apply to procedures. Do not invent an incorrect agent merely to make a sentence active; an active construction with an unspecified subject such as “something,” or a different sentence construction, may preserve the facts. |
| 3.7 | Use an approved verb to describe an action, not a noun or another part of speech. Recast nominalizations and other non-verb action expressions so the action is explicit. If the word is not an approved verb, do not use it as one; use a different sentence construction. |

## Section 4 — Sentences and clear structures

| Rule | Requirement and review detail |
|---|---|
| 4.1 | Write short, clear, accurate sentences. In procedures, state the instruction directly. In descriptive text, give one topic/idea per sentence and do not use the imperative. Avoid abstract statements that do not say what happens or what action is needed. Apply the specific limits in Sections 5 and 6. |
| 4.2 | Do not omit sentence parts or use contractions to shorten text. Include necessary nouns, verbs, subjects, and articles so the action and meaning remain clear. |
| 4.3 | Use a vertical list for complex text with many items or actions. Introduce it with a colon; identify items consistently; start items with an uppercase letter; use articles where applicable; and use periods for full-sentence items, not sentence fragments. Do not end items with commas or semicolons; end the final item with a period. Keep list levels aligned, ensure each item connects to the introductory text, and do not mix procedural and descriptive writing in one list. For safety lists, repeat “DO NOT” where needed for each prohibition. Follow applicable publication specifications for symbols and layout. |
| 4.4 | Use connecting words or phrases to show the logical relationship between related sentences. Choose approved connectors where applicable and preserve clear links in both descriptive text and procedural explanations. |
| 4.5 | Where applicable, put an article or demonstrative adjective before a noun or multi-word noun. Do not omit it for brevity. Do not add articles to general statements or abstract concepts where English does not require them; a definite article is not used before a noun with a following alphanumeric identifier. In item series, place articles so modifiers cannot be read ambiguously. |

## Section 5 — Procedural writing

| Rule | Requirement and review detail |
|---|---|
| 5.1 | Each procedural sentence, including warnings, cautions, and other safety instructions, has a maximum of 20 words under Section 8 counting rules. Notes are excluded from this limit and instead have a 25-word maximum per sentence under Rule 5.5. |
| 5.2 | Put only one instruction in each sentence unless two or more actions occur at the same time. Separate sequential actions into clearly ordered work steps. A work step may contain multiple sentences when actions occur together or a result immediately follows an action. |
| 5.3 | Write instructions in the imperative. Do not use “must” before an imperative unless the instruction is very important for safety or states an important condition. |
| 5.4 | When a condition must be known before the reader acts, put the condition first and separate it from the command with a comma. Punctuation placement must not change which action the condition modifies. |
| 5.5 | A note gives information only; it must not contain an instruction, requirement, or limit. Do not use an imperative in a note. Each note sentence may have up to 25 words. Put safety information in a warning/caution and limits or test results directly in the work step. A reader must be able to do the procedure correctly without relying on a note. Notes ordinarily belong in procedures; use them in descriptions only when necessary for an illustration or table. |

## Section 6 — Descriptive writing

| Rule | Requirement and review detail |
|---|---|
| 6.1 | Give information gradually. Keep one subject/topic in each sentence and add information in a logical sequence so readers need not reread to understand it. |
| 6.2 | Use key words and phrases consistently to connect related ideas and give the text a logical structure. Approved connecting words and phrases can show whether information is new, different, or a result. Do not change recurring terminology without reason. |
| 6.3 | Each descriptive sentence has a maximum of 25 words under Section 8 counting rules. |
| 6.4 | Use paragraphs to keep related information together and present it in logical order. Begin with a topic sentence, then explain or add information about that topic. |
| 6.5 | Keep one topic in each paragraph. The topic sentence should connect to prior information where appropriate and identify what the paragraph explains; supporting sentences should relate to that topic. |
| 6.6 | A paragraph must not have more than six grammatical sentences. List fragments do not each add a sentence: the source examples count an introduction with three list fragments as one sentence, and a preceding sentence plus an introduction with four fragments as two. Rule 8.4 uses separate units for list-item word counts. Divide longer paragraphs into smaller paragraphs without mixing topics. |

## Section 7 — Safety instructions

| Rule | Requirement and review detail |
|---|---|
| 7.1 | Use a word or applicable symbol that identifies the risk level. In the aerospace/defense definitions, a warning identifies risk of injury or death; a caution identifies risk of damage to objects. If both kinds of risk exist, use a warning. Other domains may use different labels or symbols; still apply Rules 7.1–7.3 and the applicable specifications. Base the label on an accurate risk analysis. |
| 7.2 | Start a safety instruction with a clear, accurate command or a condition the reader must know first. |
| 7.3 | Explain the risk or possible result when possible so the reader understands what can happen if the safety instruction is not obeyed. |

## Section 8 — Punctuation and word count

| Rule | Requirement and review detail |
|---|---|
| 8.1 | Standard English punctuation is permitted except the semicolon. Replace a semicolon with separate sentences as needed. |
| 8.2 | Use hyphens to connect words that are directly related, including multiword modifiers before nouns, certain numbers, letter/number-plus-noun forms, compound verbs, and vowel-ending prefixes before vowel-starting roots. A hyphen differs from a dash. |
| 8.3 | Parentheses may mark references; illustration/text item identifiers; procedural work steps; abbreviations; singular/plural alternatives; explanations; or alternatives. Use them for these functions. |
| 8.4 | For word counting in a vertical list, the introductory colon has the effect of a period. Count the text before it and each following item as separate sentence units against the applicable limit: 20 words for procedures or 25 for descriptions. This word-count treatment does not make each list fragment a separate grammatical sentence for Rule 6.6. |
| 8.5 | For the surrounding sentence's word count, all text in parentheses counts as one word. The parenthetical text also forms its own sentence when applicable, so count its words separately against that sentence's limit. |
| 8.6 | Count each number as one word; a number plus its unit as one; each abbreviation as one; each alphanumeric identifier as one; quoted text as one; and each proper noun naming an individual, group, organization, or geopolitical entity as one. Fixed titles, headings, and placard/label text that cannot be changed count as one word when referenced or quoted in a sentence. This count does not exempt editable title, heading, or label prose from its own review. Do not count paragraph or work-step numbering as words. Quoted text includes fixed uppercase/display text and formulas. |
| 8.7 | A hyphenated word or group of words counts as one word, including a hyphenated modifier and hyphenated terms used to make a long technical noun clearer. |

## Section 9 — Writing practices

| Rule | Requirement and review detail |
|---|---|
| 9.1 | Use a different sentence construction when a word-for-word replacement is unavailable, changes part of speech, is meaningless, or changes the intended meaning. Use approved words and preserve the technical message; obtain author/engineering input if meaning cannot be established. |
| 9.2 | Use each approved word with its dictionary meaning and part of speech in context. Check restricted meanings rather than relying on standard-English senses. A small number of words have more than one approved part of speech/meaning; use the context and sentence position correctly. |
| 9.3 | Do not combine approved words into a phrasal verb whose meaning differs from their approved meanings. Use a clear approved alternative. Only the small number of phrasal verbs explicitly approved in the dictionary, with their restricted meanings, are allowed. |
| 9.4 | Use consistent terminology and wording when the context and meaning are the same, especially repeated procedural actions and names for the same item. Do not vary wording in a way that can confuse readers. |

## Non-rule recommendations

Section 9 also contains General Recommendations GR-1 through GR-8. The standard says these are not rules. Consult them when relevant as recommendations, and do not report them as Rule ID violations. The recommendations cover “that,” “with,” pronouns, “this,” false friends, Latin abbreviations, inclusive language, and possessive form.
